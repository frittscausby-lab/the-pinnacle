Trading Places Explained

A comedy I watched with my dad before I understood the ending, and the real-life mechanics behind the orange juice trade.

By Fritts Causby November 21, 2026 10 min read
ShareEmailLinkedInFacebookX

As a kid, I couldn’t understand how Louis Winthorpe and Billy Ray Valentine got rich while the Dukes went poor at the same time, but I didn’t really care. When I developed an interest in trading later in life, I would lie awake at night turning it over and over in my mind.

Trading Places came out in 1983, so I was probably around 10 or 12 the first time I watched it on TV with my dad. We watched it countless times, and now that he is gone, the movie is more special to me than ever.

Spoiler alert: the following includes some crucial plot points that will ruin the movie if you’ve never seen it.

Basically, the Dukes buy orange juice futures based on a fraudulent crop report, speculating the price will climb. Winthorpe and Valentine sell short into the buying frenzy. When the real report comes out showing a normal crop, the price collapses, the heroes profit and the Dukes face a margin call that puts them in the poorhouse.

Some of my favorite quotes from the beginning of the movie are below. All three belong to Billy Ray Valentine, the street hustler played by Eddie Murphy, in his first scenes.

“I can walk! I can see!”

“Is there a problem, officers?”

“It ain’t cool being no jive turkey so close to Thanksgiving.”

To see how he ends up on the trading floor, the story has to start with the Dukes.

The Bet

When I was a kid, I had a decent understanding of the broader themes. Two powerful old men, Randolph and Mortimer Duke, decide to act as puppet masters to settle an age-old argument.

Which matters more for success in life, environment or genetics? Randolph believes the rich, well-heeled stockbroker running their firm, Winthorpe, would quickly turn to a life of crime if the rug were pulled out from under him. Mortimer thinks Winthorpe would rise above adversity because of his genetics.

“We’d have to really ruin his life though, take away his girlfriend, his job, money, everything,” Randolph says, before the brothers put the wheels in motion.

The flip side of the argument is that even a lowly street hustler could step into Winthorpe’s job and succeed if he were provided with the right environment. Randolph thinks it’s possible, Mortimer disagrees, and the brothers make a bet.

“How much do you want to bet?”

“The usual amount.”

The wager turns out to be just $1, which could actually get you something back in the 1980s.

The first step is to ruin the life of Winthorpe, who was played by Dan Aykroyd. He is an entitled jerk who never had to struggle, so it’s funny to watch him get thrown in jail, beaten up and have to pawn his watch.

The Dukes’ lackey Clarence Beeks frames Winthorpe as a thief and a drug dealer, causing him to lose his job, his home and his fiancée, Penelope. Part of the gambit included hiring Ophelia, a savvy prostitute played by Jamie Lee Curtis, to meet Winthorpe on the steps of the courthouse in front of Penelope and beg for some angel dust.

My understanding of what Ophelia did for a living was murky at best when I first saw the movie, and I did not know what angel dust was either. In short, the movie was definitely not appropriate for my age, but this was the 80s.

The second step involves bringing Valentine out of the gutter and into the firm run by the Dukes. He had been arrested earlier in the day after being wrongly accused of trying to steal money from Winthorpe. The Dukes bail him out of jail, install him in Winthorpe’s former apartment (which they own), and tell him to come to their brokerage for training first thing in the morning.

Valentine throws a rager that first night, with “Do You Wanna Funk” playing in the background, another eye-opener for me. Then, finally, he decides to retire for the evening.

The next morning, the Dukes provide an overview of commodities trading. Their explanation of how clients speculate on the movement of prices and buy/sell to other individuals to generate capital was simple enough for a youngster like me to grasp. And the best part of their business, according to the Dukes, is that “because of commissions, they always win, regardless of whether their clients lose money or make money.”

Valentine: “Sounds to me like you guys are just a couple of bookies.”

Randolph: “I told you he’d understand.” Mortimer issues a harrumph.

The business model of the Dukes, of course, was the standard on Wall Street until the rise of commission-free trading, but I digress.

Valentine turns out to be a quick study and a rising star at the firm. It is not long, however, before he and Winthorpe learn about the bet and set out to turn the tables on the Dukes.

The Train

The scene on the New Year’s Eve train was one of my favorites back when I was young, 300 million years ago. Winthorpe and Valentine are there in disguise to try to steal the crop report from Beeks. They are joined in their efforts by Winthorpe’s butler, Coleman, who is dressed up as a drunk Irish priest, and by Ophelia, who is dressed in a very enticing (for me as a teenager), extremely low-cut set of lederhosen.

Beeks figures out the plot and pulls a gun. The heroes are bailed out when a gorilla being transported on the train knocks him out. They put Beeks in a gorilla suit and cage him up with the gorilla, which I found hilarious.

Many of the jokes appeal to a younger audience and there is also an intelligent theme running under the surface, which is part of why the movie is a classic. Beyond the nature-versus-nurture argument, the ending is complex for those who do not have trading knowledge.

One of the most iconic scenes of the movie is at the end, when Winthorpe and Valentine are on the floor of the exchange surrounded by a panicked, shouting group of traders. After the chaos finally abates, the Dukes are left with nothing and Mortimer has a panic attack. The reason why they lost their fortune was a mystery to me until I started actively trading and learning about the markets.

The Trade in Plain English

The Dukes paid Beeks to procure an early copy of the US Department of Agriculture’s orange crop report before it went public. The report was critical because of its impact on the price of frozen concentrated orange juice, a futures contract that represents a promise to buy or sell at a certain price on a certain date.

Scarcity is the result of a bad harvest, meaning the price will be higher. Abundance is the result of a good harvest, meaning the price will be lower.

Winthorpe and Valentine were able to swap the real report for a fake when they were on the train. The fake they swapped in for the real report predicted a bad crop. After Winthorpe and Valentine passed the fake to the Dukes by posing as Beeks in a dimly lit parking garage, they operated under the conclusion that prices would skyrocket.

The Dukes ordered their trader to buy as many orange juice contracts as possible. Other traders witnessed the action and piled into the trade, causing the price to rise to $1.42 a pound.

Valentine gets nervous watching the price increase. Winthorpe tells him to hold on.

“Not yet, almost.”

Finally, they sold contracts into the frenzy. This is a short sale, a bet that the price will fall. When the Secretary of Agriculture announced the real report stating the crop was robust, the price cratered, falling to 29 cents a pound. Winthorpe and Valentine bought their contracts back at the bottom, closed out their promises and pocketed the difference.

The Dukes bought at the top and now hold contracts worth a fraction of what they paid. Since futures are leveraged, a small deposit controls a large position. If the price of a futures contract falls, the exchange asks for more cash to cover the loss. This is known as a margin call and for investors that do not have enough cash on hand, the result is a forced liquidation.

In the film, the Dukes’ losses amount to nearly $400 million. They cannot pay, so the exchange seizes all their assets.

The Dukes finally realized what happened when they saw Winthorpe and Valentine celebrating.

“How could you do this to us, after everything we’ve done for you?”

Valentine (elated): “Louis bet me that we couldn’t both get rich and put y’all in the poorhouse at the same time, he didn’t think we could do it!”

Winthorpe: “I lost.” Then he hands Valentine a crisp $1 bill.

Why Trading Places Confused Me

If the mechanics feel slippery, there is a good reason. Part of the confusion is built into the story. According to Slashfilm, screenwriter Herschel Weingrod suggested viewers got lost because the heroes reversed the classic buy low, sell high playbook. The director John Landis admitted it took him a long time to understand the con.

The ending is based on the premise that it is possible to sell something that is borrowed, that a forged document can move a crowd, and that leverage can result in financial ruin.

Trading Places is rare in that it was shot on a real trading floor. The exchange scenes were filmed on the COMEX floor inside 4 World Trade Center in New York. Wikipedia reported that most of the people on screen were real traders and that the shoot was moved to a weekend after the cast’s presence on a weekday disrupted active trading.

Legal Changes from Trading Places? Really?

As an adult, I wondered if the plotline had any factual elements. Truth is stranger than fiction, and the nuances of the movie seemed too complex to have been pulled out of thin air. I love movies based on true stories (see my article on Bottle Shock) and although this one is not, the Dukes were loosely based on real people.

According to Wikipedia, the idea originated from writer Timothy Harris’ tennis games with two wealthy brothers who were also very frugal. The brothers were highly competitive and often bet against each other. The writers used research covering the Hunt brothers’ silver squeeze and real stories from the financial markets, including attempts that had been made to corner the wheat market.

One of the more surprising outcomes from the movie did not happen until decades later in 2010, when Congress passed the Eddie Murphy rule.

The Eddie Murphy Rule

Trading commodities off a leaked government report was not clearly illegal when Trading Places came out in 1983. The idea that it should be was clearly important to Gary Gensler, however, as he cited the film in testimony around the time that the Dodd-Frank Act was passed in 2010.

Gensler was chairman of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) when the Dodd-Frank Act was passed. Section 746 of the Act became known as the Eddie Murphy rule, as it bars trading commodities on misappropriated government information.

Eddie Murphy has a long list of accolades associated with his name, of course, though I imagine it still must be flattering to have an Act that is nicknamed after you. One can only wonder if Dan Aykroyd felt slighted.

Life Lessons

The movie changed the law and the shape of my life. Aside from introducing me to Jamie Lee, which was a big deal at the time, it was the catalyst for my interest in trading.

Trading seemed cool and exciting. Even at a young age, I saw the potential of the markets and how investing can be a pathway to building wealth. The scene where the Dukes explained their business made the concepts easy to understand, and the complexity of the ending fed my fascination with the markets. It is one of many reasons why I’ve seen it so many times.

Information is an asset and I often think about the movie when a report moves the market. The psychology of the crowd moves the market, and that has become more interesting to me as I have gained experience. The Dukes were making very large purchases, so other people piled in and the price moved because of the herd mentality. If you got in when the Dukes started buying, then had the sense to not be a pig and get out, you were fine. Bulls make money and bears make money, but pigs get slaughtered.

The momentum trade is a real strategy and it can build fortunes, but markets move up on an escalator and take the elevator down. Understanding when interest is waning and it is time to get out is a real skill—something I’m still working on (ask me about Netflix).

The Dukes’ ruin clearly illustrates the downside to using leverage, which I explored in a previous article. Leverage compounds gains but it can also bring storm clouds. And as anyone who has ever been forced to liquidate positions can attest, it can certainly spoil an afternoon.

If you have an interest in the markets and have never seen Trading Places, consider watching it soon because it is worth the time. The plot is maybe too complicated for an 80s comedy, but that is also why it holds up. And if you’re like me and the movie is tied to memories of Christmas and family time, it’s comforting to have it on in the background. I sure wish I could watch it with my dad again.
